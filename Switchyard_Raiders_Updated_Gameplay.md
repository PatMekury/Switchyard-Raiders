# Switchyard Raiders: Updated Gameplay

## 1. What the players are doing

Switchyard Raiders is a seated tabletop multiplayer game about intercepting freight trains in a miniature town. Each player controls a locomotive. Rival teams have separate homes in the town, and company trains carry valuable cargo along the railway between streets, through tunnels, and over hills.

Players place single-use decouplers beside the railway, predict when a useful coupling will pass, and activate their devices during a short firing window. A successful shot disconnects cargo from its train. Players then use the railway, including connections they have placed at designated locations, to reach the loose wagons, attach them to their own locomotive, and deliver them home.

**The complete repeating loop is: watch the freight, choose a place to intercept it, prepare a collection route, place and aim a decoupler, fire at the right moment, collect the released cargo, and bring it home. Then prepare another interception.**

The first mode is 1v1, with one locomotive per player. The later 2v2 mode gives every player their own locomotive and gives each team a shared delivery objective. Both sides act at the same time.

This document describes the updated gameplay. Numerical settings and a small number of unresolved rules are identified where they affect play.

## 2. A complete 1v1 play sequence

### Starting at home

You begin with your locomotive at your team's home. Your opponent starts at the other home. You can identify your locomotive from its team colour, emblem, and personal number, which also appear on your control tray.

The opening freight opportunity gives both players comparable travel time to an interception location. Neither player should gain the first wagon simply because their home is closer to its route.

You look across the town and see a company train approaching a hill. It has two cargo wagons. You can see its direction of travel, the couplings between its vehicles, and the tracks that could carry released cargo away.

### Planning the interception

You decide to disconnect the rear wagon while the company train is climbing. That wagon will initially continue uphill, then slow and roll back down if the slope is sufficient.

Before taking the shot, you identify how your own locomotive will reach it. A suitable connection location joins your route to the lower part of the hill. You place the compatible track piece there and check the direction arrow showing where your locomotive will emerge.

You move your locomotive to the collection position. For this interception, it faces downhill with its rear coupling toward the slope. The intended result is for the released wagon to approach that rear coupling from uphill.

This preparation matters. Disconnecting a wagon without a usable collection route can leave it available to your opponent.

### Setting the decoupler

You take a decoupler from your tray and place it on suitable ground beside the uphill track. You swivel its head toward the coupling between the two cargo wagons.

As that coupling approaches the device's firing area, the bar above the decoupler fills. The selected device's bar is also visible on your tray. You keep watching the train and activate the device when the bar is full.

The device fires once. If your aim and timing are correct, the coupling opens. The company engine continues uphill with the front wagon. The rear wagon is now separate. Your fired decoupler remains visibly spent at its location.

### Collecting the released wagon

The loose wagon continues briefly uphill, slows, and rolls back down. This example uses a gentle slope and a short collection distance, allowing a controlled coupling approach.

Your locomotive is already positioned on the same connected track, with its free rear coupling facing the approaching wagon. You use the brake or movement control to obtain a slow, aligned contact. The couplings meet and latch. Only now does the wagon become part of your train.

Your opponent could have collected it first if they had prepared a better position. Firing the successful decoupler does not reserve the cargo for you.

### Choosing the next move

You now have one wagon. You can return home to secure it, or remain in the town and attempt another collection before returning.

Keeping the wagon with you creates a new risk: your opponent can place a decoupler along your likely return route. A second cargo wagon also makes your train longer, so your route and available manoeuvring space matter more.

You choose to go home. At the next junction, you notice an enemy decoupler beside the direct route. You can choose another available route or change your approach speed to disrupt the opponent's expected timing. A speed change does not cancel the threat; the device's opportunity still depends on your coupling actually entering its firing area.

### Delivering and returning

You follow the railway into your home. Once the complete cargo chain is inside the unloading area, it is unloaded and adds to your team's delivered total. Before it reaches that protected area, the cargo remains vulnerable to an interception.

Your locomotive is now free to return to the town. You inspect the next freight opportunity, select another unused decoupler if one is available, and prepare the next interception.

Each interception can end differently: you collect your own cut, your opponent collects it, a wagon rolls toward a better position, or you miss the firing window and must reconsider your plan.

## 3. The town and its railway

The play space is a miniature town with recognisable locations: team homes, company railway entrances, streets, tunnel entrances, hill sections, junctions, and stretches of parallel track. The railway's shape determines where players can intercept and collect cargo.

### Hills

Loose cargo keeps its motion when disconnected. The slope then affects what happens next:

- On an uphill section, a released wagon initially continues uphill. It slows and can roll backward downhill.
- On a downhill section, it continues downhill and can gain speed.
- On a level section, it coasts and slows through rolling resistance.

The cargo follows its rails throughout this movement. Cutting it does not move it onto a nearby parallel track.

Players must be able to read the slope from the town's shape. A hill can help a player bring cargo toward a waiting locomotive, but it can also carry cargo toward an opponent or away from a planned collection point.

A fast collision is not the same as a successful coupling. The collection manoeuvre requires compatible ends, alignment, and a controlled contact speed. Collision consequences beyond that requirement remain undecided.

### Tunnels

Trains continue moving through tunnels. Tunnel entrances and exits offer places to anticipate when a train will disappear and reappear.

A player can prepare a decoupler near an exit, but needs a clear shot when the desired coupling emerges. Terrain blocks the shot; a device cannot fire through a hill to reach a coupling inside the tunnel.

The town should make tunnel routes understandable so that a player can plan around an exit rather than guess where a hidden train will appear.

### Team homes and equal openings

Each team has a recognisable home connected to the railway. Its colours and emblem match that team's locomotives and control trays. A home provides a starting location and a delivery destination.

The starting positions should have equal railway distance to equivalent interception points on the opening cargo route. Their travel times must also be comparable: slopes, junctions, train speed, and the cargo's arrival time all affect that opportunity. Equal straight-line distance between homes and cargo is insufficient by itself.

## 4. Placing, aiming, and firing a decoupler

The decoupler is a device placed on the ground beside a track. Its shot releases a coupling, making the cargo beyond that connection separate from the rest of the train.

### Placement

You take an unused decoupler from your personal tray and choose a suitable location beside the railway. The placement preview must show whether the ground position and firing clearance are valid.

The device needs room beside the track. It cannot be placed inside a train, on an occupied rail, or behind terrain that would prevent the intended shot.

You decide its position before the desired coupling passes. That position determines which passing trains and couplings you can threaten.

### Aiming

You swivel the decoupler's head left or right to aim toward the intended coupling. Its direction remains visible after placement.

The firing bar concerns the coupling being aimed at. It must not become ready simply because the front of a long locomotive has arrived while the intended cargo is still outside the firing area.

### The firing window

As the intended coupling approaches, the bar fills. While the bar is full, the device can be manually activated. Once that coupling leaves the valid firing area, its window closes.

A full bar means that the shot can be attempted. It does not guarantee a hit or correct poor aim. The device does not fire automatically when the bar fills.

The player can activate the selected device from the nearby control tray. This allows attention to remain on the passing train rather than requiring a last-second reach across the town.

### The result

After an actual shot, the device is spent whether the projectile hits or misses. It cannot fire a second shot. Its appearance changes so nobody confuses it with an unused threat.

On a hit, the targeted connection visibly opens and the released wagons continue according to their motion and the slope. On a miss, the train remains coupled.

**Unresolved rule:** if the player misses the full-bar window without firing, the device remains where it was placed. Whether it is then permanently spent or can wait for a later train has not been decided. The gameplay does not assume either outcome.

The starting quantity of decouplers and the way players obtain replacements also remain undecided. Every additional shot requires an unused device.

## 5. Connecting parallel tracks with C and Y pieces

Players carry track pieces that fit designated connection locations. These locations exist where the town has enough clear space and compatible track heights for a connection to form.

The pieces let players prepare useful approaches to cargo. They are particularly relevant when two locomotives are travelling in opposite directions on parallel tracks.

### C-shaped connections

A suitable C-shaped return curve can take a locomotive around a bend and send it back along a parallel track in the opposite direction.

The entire train must follow the curve. The connection therefore needs enough space for the locomotive and its attached wagons, not just the front of the engine.

### Y-shaped connections

A Y-shaped junction joins or separates routes. It can provide a branch from one route toward another where the designated location supports that geometry.

It does not automatically turn a locomotive around. The direction in which the train emerges depends on the actual rails and the branch it takes.

### Placing a connection

1. Take a compatible piece from the personal tray.
2. Select a designated connection location.
3. Inspect the preview, including the outgoing direction arrow.
4. Place the piece when the track area is clear.
5. Choose the new branch when routing the locomotive through that junction.

The preview must make the result understandable before commitment. A connection cannot appear, disappear, or change beneath a train. Placement must also be blocked if an approaching train has already committed to that section.

A connection creates a rail route. It does not pull cargo sideways onto the player's track, turn a wagon in place, or guarantee possession of nearby cargo.

The inventory, lifetime, removal rules, and access rights for player-placed connections remain undecided.

## 6. Locomotive movement and cargo attachment

### Following the railway

A locomotive follows its rails automatically once moving. The player controls movement, braking, direction, and the available branch at a junction. Continuous steering is unnecessary.

Forward and reverse movement are included in this updated gameplay. Reversing is important for positioning and adding cargo to an existing chain. The locomotive changes travel direction without instantly rotating its body.

A reverse manoeuvre requires clear track behind the entire train. It cannot move through another locomotive or push attached wagons through an occupied junction. Normal traffic on opposing parallel tracks remains separate until a valid connection joins those routes.

### One continuous cargo chain

Cargo attaches in one continuous chain at the locomotive's designated cargo end. Additional wagons extend the free end of the existing chain.

```text
First collection:       Locomotive - A
Second collection:      Locomotive - A - B
Third collection:       Locomotive - A - B - C
```

These diagrams explain attachment order; they do not set a three-wagon carrying capacity.

The arrangement does not become `B - Locomotive - A`. A player does not collect a new wagon at the locomotive's other end simply because it happens to make contact there.

When the locomotive travels forward, it pulls its chain. When it reverses, it pushes that same chain. The attachment points stay where they are.

### Adding a wagon when already carrying cargo

Suppose your train is `Locomotive - A`, and B is loose farther along the railway.

You first need a route that puts your complete train ahead of B, facing away from it, with A's free coupling toward B. A passing track and a suitable connection can provide that route.

You then reverse slowly. A approaches B, their compatible couplings meet, and the result is `Locomotive - A - B`.

If no route lets you achieve that alignment, the pickup is not currently available. You must use another connection, wait for the cargo to reach a different position, or choose a different target. The game cannot solve the manoeuvre by snapping B across rails or through A.

### Collecting rolling cargo

On a slope, the player can instead arrange for a loose wagon to roll toward the free end of their train. The same coupling requirements apply: connected rails, compatible ends, alignment, and a controlled relative speed.

Choosing the cutting point and collection position together is therefore part of the robbery.

## 7. How many wagons a company train carries

Company trains can carry multiple cargo wagons. The working starting configuration is two cargo wagons per company train. A single wagon is sufficient when introducing the first interception.

For this train:

```text
Company locomotive - A - B
```

Cutting the connection between the company locomotive and A releases A and B as a connected group. Cutting the connection between A and B releases only B.

The player chooses between freeing a larger group and freeing one easier-to-handle wagon. On a hill, the location of the cut also determines the group's likely movement after release.

The same connection rule applies to a player's cargo chain. Cutting nearer the locomotive can release several wagons; cutting the final connection releases only the last wagon.

The maximum carrying capacity of a player locomotive has not been set for this updated version. A connected group must fit that eventual capacity before it can be collected as a whole.

## 8. Controls while moving and resizing the town

The player has a personal control tray positioned comfortably in front of or beside their seated position. It can be repositioned separately from the town.

The town can be lifted, moved, rotated, enlarged, and reduced for inspection and interaction. The tray remains at its chosen position and retains comfortable control sizes while the town changes size or position.

### What stays in the town

- Locomotives and cargo wagons.
- Team homes and delivery areas.
- Railway tracks, connection locations, and placed track pieces.
- Placed decouplers, their aiming heads, and their firing bars.
- Hills, buildings, streets, and tunnels.

### What stays on the personal tray

- The player's train identity and team delivery total.
- Movement, brake/stop, and forward/reverse controls.
- A clear choice for an available branch at the next junction.
- Available decouplers and C/Y track pieces.
- Selection and activation of the player's placed decoupler, with its matching firing bar.

Selecting a device highlights it in the town and identifies it on the tray. If more than one unused device has been placed, the player must be able to tell which one the activation control will fire.

### Hands-only interaction

Nearby pieces can be grabbed and placed into suitable locations. A player can also point to a distant valid location and confirm it with a pinch, avoiding a reach across an enlarged town.

The decoupler head can be swivelled while preparing the shot. The actual firing command remains a brief, deliberate input on the selected device's tray control.

Train controls remain in their accepted state when the player releases them. Keeping a locomotive moving does not require holding a hand in the air continuously.

Grabbing the town and operating a gameplay object must be distinct actions. Moving the model must not accidentally fire a selected decoupler or place a track piece.

### Viewing does not alter the shared match

Each player adjusts their own view of the town. Their adjustment does not move another player's view.

Resizing the town does not shorten travel times, extend a decoupler's gameplay range, or change cargo speed. Tilting the model for inspection does not change the direction of gravity on its hills. All players continue to share the same railway positions and gameplay events.

The home remains part of the town. Moving its image closer to the player does not bring it closer to a locomotive travelling on the railway.

## 9. Identifying trains, cargo, and homes

Each team has a distinct colour and emblem. Those identifiers appear on its home, locomotives, and personal trays. Individual locomotives also carry a large number, allowing teammates to distinguish their vehicles in 2v2.

The matching identity on the tray establishes which locomotive that player controls, even after the town has been rotated or resized.

Cargo retains its recognisable cargo appearance. A small ownership marker appears when it is physically connected to a player's train. That marker disappears when the cargo becomes loose.

The player who fired a successful shot receives no automatic ownership. A wagon becomes part of a player's haul through physical collection, and becomes a delivered point through unloading at that team's home.

## 10. Delivery, scoring, and the end of a match

The working 1v1 objective is to deliver three cargo wagons to your home before the opponent does. Each cargo wagon counts as one delivery.

Carrying cargo is not yet a point. The complete cargo chain must enter the home's unloading area before delivery is secured. Cargo disconnected on the public approach remains in play.

Unloading removes the delivered cargo from the locomotive and adds it to the team's visible total. The locomotive can then leave home for another collection.

The match ends when a team reaches the delivery target. The winning total and the delivered wagons make the result visible.

The precise freight supply, arrival schedule, match time limit, and time-limit tie rule have not been set for this updated version. Those settings must leave enough cargo and interception opportunities for the delivery objective to remain achievable.

## 11. Four-player gameplay: 2v2

Each team has two players, two independently controlled locomotives, and one shared home and delivery total. Every player retains the ability to drive, place a decoupler, fire it, place a compatible track connection, collect cargo, and deliver it.

Cooperation comes from dividing the preparation and collection work according to each train's position.

### Cutting for a teammate

One player places and aims a decoupler beside a hill. Their teammate positions a locomotive downhill with its free coupling facing the expected cargo route. The first player makes the cut; the second collects the rolling wagon and delivers it.

The cutter does not need to race their own locomotive to the same pickup location. The teammate's earlier positioning makes the plan possible.

### Preparing a route for a loaded train

One player carries cargo toward home. Their teammate places a compatible connection at a clear designated location, creating a useful route around an anticipated interception. The loaded player still chooses the branch and drives through it.

A teammate's placement changes the available railway; it does not take control of the other player's locomotive.

### Taking separate opportunities

Two teammates can prepare different interception points or collect different released wagons. They should be able to communicate which cargo each is approaching so that both do not unintentionally converge on the same coupling.

The four-player delivery target may be higher than the 1v1 target. Increasing it also requires sufficient cargo supply and useful interception opportunities. The exact target and arrival rate remain undecided.

## 12. Where the tension and laughter arise

The main tension comes from committing to a plan before its result is secure. A player chooses a location, uses a device, watches the bar fill, and decides when to fire. The cut then creates a new contest over where the cargo will travel and who can collect it.

Examples of understandable reversals include:

- You make a clean cut, but your opponent has already positioned their locomotive where the cargo rolls.
- You prepare for one coupling, then realise that a different cut would have released the wagon you wanted.
- An opponent changes speed near your decoupler, disrupting the moment at which you expected to activate it.
- A teammate times the cut correctly and sends a wagon directly into your prepared collection position.
- You stay out for another wagon while your opponent secures a smaller delivery and takes the lead.
- You reveal a track connection that makes your planned interception route obvious, giving the opponent time to respond.

These moments depend on visible causes: location, slope, route, timing, aim, and train position. Players should be able to explain what happened and make their next decision immediately.

## Tabletop integration decisions (2026-10-22)

The user has now specified a six-delivery team target for 2v2. Wagons should appear gradually, and freight should continue replenishing until a team delivers six. This supersedes the previously undecided four-player target and finite freight supply. The current integration uses one incoming wagon every 20 seconds with six active cargo slots recycled after delivery; that interval is provisional rather than a finalized balancing rule.
